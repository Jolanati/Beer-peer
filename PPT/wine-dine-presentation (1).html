01 · The Problem
You know what you're eating.
But what do you drink with it?

You're at a restaurant. There's a dish you love. The wine list has forty options and none of them mean anything to you. You pick something vaguely familiar, hope for the best, and move on.

The situation
Wine pairing feels like a skill you either have — or don't.

Most people don't feel confident choosing wine to match food. The knowledge gap is real, and it's intimidating.

The current options
Ask the sommelier. Google it. Or just order the house wine.

None of these start from what actually matters — what the food tastes like, right now, on your plate.

The idea
What if the only thing you needed was a photo?

Wine & Dine removes the gap entirely. Photograph your dish. The system does the rest — no wine knowledge required.

03 · Data Preparation
Before training anything —
clean, split, freeze.
Data Cleaning — WineSensed
Missing fields removed
Reviews with no text, grape, wine name, or rating — unusable for training
Zero-rated entries dropped
Vivino encodes "not yet rated" as 0.0 — not a real score
Short reviews removed
Fewer than 5 words carry no meaningful signal for the model
Non-English filtered out
Non-Latin script produces near-zero vectors in a model trained on English
Duplicates removed
Vivino reattaches the same note to multiple vintages — biases class distribution

Food-101 required no cleaning — curated benchmark, no missing labels.

Train / Validate / Test Split
70%

15%

15%
test

Stratified split — every portion preserves the same class proportions. Applied to both datasets independently.

Why split before EDA?

EDA was run on training data only. Computing statistics on the full dataset would let test-set information quietly influence architecture decisions — a subtle but real form of data leakage.

04 · The Models — Text Processing
Teaching a model to read wine reviews.
From LSTM to BiLSTM with attention.
LSTM baseline
Unidirectional LSTM trained on 824k WineSensed reviews to classify 15 grape varieties.
16.4%
top-1 accuracy
Bidirectional + Bahdanau attention
BiLSTM + Bahdanau attention
Processes each review forwards and backwards. Attention weights highlight tasting words — "cassis", "grippy tannin" — over filler. + DistilBERT bonus (+3 pts).
21.9%
top-1 accuracy
Is 21.9% a good result?

Random chance across 15 classes is 6.7% — so the model is 3x better than guessing. But a naive classifier always predicting Cabernet Sauvignon (the most common grape) would score around ~29% from class imbalance alone.

So the grape classification accuracy is modest. But it was never the real goal.

What actually matters

The model trained on grape labels, but what we needed was the hidden state — the internal representation the BiLSTM built of wine review language. That representation is the raw material for the next step. Grape classification accuracy turned out to be the wrong question entirely.

05 · The Bridge
We had two capable models.
Now we needed a bridge.

Food photo → ResNet-50 → "Cheesecake"

Wine review → BiLSTM → "Sangiovese"

Two completely different vocabularies, two completely different models. We needed something in between. So we tried. And tried again.

Eight attempts before we found the answer.

05 · The Bridge — First Attempt
Word2Vec. The obvious first bridge.
It collapsed immediately.
The assumption

Food and wine share vocabulary — creamy, butter, earthy. W2V already trained on 824k reviews knew wine language well. Encode the food description, find the nearest wine words by cosine similarity.

"creamy cheesecake" → W2V → cosine sim → "buttery Chardonnay"
What actually happened

All 101 food queries collapsed into the same vector region. Pizza and cheesecake both got Sangiovese.

Word-level similarity is not sentence-level understanding. Food vocabulary like "charred yeasty crust" has no meaningful wine neighbours in W2V space.

Why W2V still mattered

05 · The Bridge — Six More Attempts
We tried six more ways to match food to grape.
Can an already trained grape classifier just pick the right grape from a food description?
Why we thought so
The model was already trained on grape vocabulary — an argmax on its output should give a direct recommendation.
How it worked
It did produce recommendations — but one grape dominated 44 out of 101 foods.
Why it failed
Training imbalance meant the model defaulted to the most common grape regardless of what food was given.
What if we skip the label — and use the encoder's hidden state instead?
Why we thought so
The hidden state carries richer information than the final class output — it should reflect flavor, not just grape rank.
How it worked
All 15 grape centroids were nearly identical — pairwise cosine 0.85–0.99.
Why it failed
Cross-entropy training collapses all embeddings toward decision boundaries — at every layer.
Is the bottleneck how we describe the food — what if we write it like a wine reviewer?
Why we thought so
If the food description uses sensory language that matches the training vocabulary, it should route more precisely.
How it worked
9 out of 10 foods changed their recommendation — input format genuinely matters.
Why it failed
Better routing can't fix a collapsed embedding space. The diversity ceiling was still there.
What if we use MPNET — an encoder designed to actually spread categories apart?
Why we thought so
MPNET was contrastively trained to push different meanings apart — so its grape centroids would not collapse.
How it worked
Grape centroids did spread well (cosine 0.03–0.70) — but food keywords landed in completely the wrong region.
Why it failed
Good encoder geometry doesn't help if food and wine don't share the same semantic neighbourhood.
Can we steer MPNET's space with a small trained head — without retraining the whole model?
Why we thought so
A lightweight projection layer trained with contrastive loss should pull food and wine closer without touching the backbone.
How it worked
The head learned a transformation — but the underlying backbone geometry remained exactly the same.
Why it failed
The backbone defines the space. A layer on top cannot reorganize what is already baked into the weights.
What if we combine MPNET's better geometry with wine-style food descriptions — surely that's enough?
Why we thought so
Two improvements combined — a better encoder that spreads, and richer input that bridges vocabulary — should close the gap.
How it worked
5 out of 10 grapes reached — an improvement — but completely different grapes than BiLSTM suggested.
Why it failed
Domain mismatch confirmed. No encoder trained on grape labels can align food and wine in a shared meaningful space.
...and they all failed.
05 · The Turn
Stop asking what grape.
Start asking what taste.

The problem with grape labels is that they are names — not descriptions. Cabernet Sauvignon can be a hundred different taste experiences depending on terroir, vintage, and winemaker. The label compresses all of that into one point.

What if we trained on what wine actually tastes like — not what grape it is?

06 · The Architecture — TasteBiLSTM
A BiLSTM trained on taste, not grape.
The encoder at the heart of the system.
The encoding pipeline
1   Input
Wine review text → tokenised → Word2Vec embeddings
2   BiLSTM
Bidirectional LSTM processes sequence in both directions simultaneously
3   Attention
Bahdanau attention weights highlight the most taste-relevant tokens
4   512-d vector
Final hidden state → L2-normalised taste embedding
5   Multi-label
Trained on 10 sensory axes simultaneously — not 15 grape labels
Dense, creamy New York-style cheesecake with a buttery graham cracker crust, rich cream cheese filling, and a silky vanilla sweetness with just enough lemon tang. darker = higher attention weight
Why This Works
Taste spreads vectors. Grape training compresses to boundaries. Taste training spreads across a continuous sensory manifold.
Cosine range. Grape-based: 0.85–0.99. Taste-based: −0.097 to 0.841, mean 0.391. Real distance = real discrimination.
Shared space. Food descriptions encoded by the same TasteBiLSTM live in the same space as wine vectors. No translation layer needed.
06 · The Architecture — Clustering & Retrieval
BisectingKMeans discovers 9 flavor worlds.
Cosine similarity does the rest.

100k+ wine taste vectors clustered into 9 flavor neighborhoods. Cluster names are assigned automatically by TF-IDF — the most distinctive vocabulary of each cluster determines its name. No label is ever manually assigned.
