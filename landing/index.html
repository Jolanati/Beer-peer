Chapter 1 · The Problem

You know what you're eating.
But what do you drink with it?

You're at a restaurant. The wine list has forty options and none of them mean anything to you. You pick something vaguely familiar, hope for the best, and move on.

The situation

Wine pairing feels like a skill you either have — or don't.

Most people don't feel confident choosing wine to match food. The knowledge gap is real, and it's intimidating.

Current options

Ask the sommelier. Google it. Or just order the house wine.

None of these start from what actually matters — what the food tastes like, right now, on your plate.

Our idea

What if the only thing you needed was a photo?

Wine & Dine removes the gap entirely. Photograph your dish. The system does the rest — no wine knowledge required.

Chapter 3 · Preparation

Preparing the data

Step 1 — Filter

WineSensed cleaning

From 824,000 raw tasting notes, five passes remove unusable rows.

  • Missing essentials —no text, grape, wine name, or rating
  • Zero-rated —Vivino encodes "not yet rated" as 0.0
  • Short reviews —fewer than 5 words, no useful signal
  • Non-English —less than 80% ASCII characters
  • Duplicates —same note reattached to multiple vintages

Food-101 needs no cleaning — torchvision delivers 1,000 images × 101 classes, perfectly balanced.

Step 2 — Split

70 · 15 · 15

Both datasets split with a fixed seed, before any EDA — test-set statistics never touch architecture decisions.

70% Train
15% Val
15% Test

Stratified by target class. Test set frozen — touched exactly once per model.

Step 3 — Balance

Class imbalance

The two datasets present opposite challenges.

Food-101
Perfectly balanced 101 classes · 1,000 images each · no mitigation needed
WineSensed
Imbalanced Top grape has 10× more reviews than the rarest — weighted cross-entropy gives rare classes stronger gradients

Chapter 5 · Computer Vision

Teaching a machine to see food

Accuracy on 101 food categories

First attempt — custom CNN 4-block network built from scratch, random weights
48%
ResNet-50 — transfer learning Pretrained on 1.2M ImageNet photos, adapted for food
76.5%

Top-1 accuracy · 15,150 unseen test images · 101 food categories

How we got there

1
Built our own — then hit the ceiling A VGG-style network with 4 convolutional blocks, trained for 20 epochs. Reached 48% — impressive for a model that learned purely from our 70k training photos, but not production-ready.
2
Borrowed a giant's eyes ResNet-50 already "knows" textures, edges, and shapes from 1.2M images. We replaced only its final layer — now it answers "which of 101 dishes?" instead of "which of 1,000 ImageNet objects?".
3
Two-phase fine-tuning Phase A (5 epochs): train the new head only — let it stabilise before touching anything else. Phase B (10 epochs): gently unfreeze the deep layers so the model adapts its visual intuition to food specifically. Result: 76.5% accuracy, F1 0.764.

Chapter 9 · Six More Doors

We tried six more things

What we tried
What happened
BiLSTM argmax "Our model already knows grapes — just ask it to pick one for each food"
It always chose the same one. One grape won 44 of 101 dishes.
BiLSTM centroid cosine "Don't ask for a pick — compare the flavor fingerprint of food to each grape"
All grape fingerprints looked nearly identical. You can't rank things that look the same.
BiLSTM + wine descriptions "Describe the food the way a sommelier would, not a chef"
Different picks — but the fingerprints were still too close.
MPNET + keywords "Use a newer, stronger language model that separates things better"
Grape fingerprints spread apart — but food words landed in the wrong region entirely.
MPNET + SupCon projection head "Train a layer on top to actively push grapes apart and pull food toward the right ones"
The base was frozen — its grape fingerprints didn't move. We were reshaping shadows, not the wall.
MPNET + wine descriptions "Combine both fixes — stronger model and sommelier language"
5 of 10 unique grapes. Closer. Still not enough.

Seven doors. Same wall.

We weren't failing at the methods.

We were asking the wrong question.

Chapter 10 · The Turn

Stop asking what grape.
Start asking what taste.

The problem with grape labels is that they are names — not descriptions. Cabernet Sauvignon can be a hundred different taste experiences depending on terroir, vintage, and winemaker. The label compresses all of that into one point.

What if we trained on what wine actually tastes like — not what grape it is?

Chapter 11 · The Taste Language

Teaching a model what flavor means

1 Decide what taste means

We picked 10 dimensions that cover how wine tastes: body, acidity, tannin, red fruit, fruit, earthy, sweet, oaky, floral, mineral. Think of them as 10 dials on a flavor control panel.

Multi-label classification targets
2 Write a cheat sheet for each dial

For each dimension we listed the words that signal it. Oaky = oak, vanilla, toast, cedar, smoky. Written by hand — no model involved yet.

Keyword seed lists for weak supervision
3 Scan 824,000 reviews with those cheat sheets

Like a highlighter on every review. If it contains any oaky word → oaky fires. Multiple dials fire at once. Each review ends up with its own flavor signature — automatically, no human labeling needed.

Weak supervision → binary 10-dim label vectors
4 Train a model to recognize taste from text

We showed the neural network thousands of labeled reviews. It learned: when you see this language, these dials fire. After training it can read any text and predict the flavor profile on its own.

TasteBiLSTM — BiLSTM + Bahdanau attention, 10-axis output head

Chapter 12 · From Text to Taste Space

Every wine gets a taste address

5 The model learns more than 10 dials

The 10 axes told the model what to learn. Internally it built a finer picture — 512 numbers per text. Similar tastes produce similar numbers. Different tastes point away from each other.

512-d L2-normalised hidden state, cosine range –0.097 to 0.841
6 Stamp every wine with its taste address

We ran all 824,000 wine reviews through the trained model. Each wine became a point in taste space — placed by how it tastes, not by grape name or price.

TasteBiLSTM encoder inference on full corpus
7 Group wines into taste neighborhoods

The math suggested 12 groups. We chose 9 — one per taste dimension — because it makes results explainable and avoids splitting real flavor profiles into noise.

BisectingKMeans K=9, silhouette 0.4757 vs K=12 at 0.4972
8 Let the machine name the neighborhoods

We ran a text analysis on each group to find the most distinctive words. The machine named them automatically: "Something Bold", "Something Sweet", "Something Earthy & Smoky"...

TF-IDF on cluster review texts → top keyword → cluster name

Chapter 13 · The Bridge

Food and wine — same space, same language

9 Write one taste sentence per food

For every dish we wrote a plain English description of how it tastes — the same kind of language a wine review uses. "Warm, buttery, cinnamon-spiced, with a sweet caramelized edge." Written by an AI language model.

LLM-generated descriptions — Claude Sonnet
10 Send food through the same encoder

The food description goes through the exact same model. It gets the same kind of 512-number taste address. Now food and wine live in the same space.

Cross-modal retrieval via shared embedding space

For the first time, a strawberry tart and a Pinot Noir can be neighbors — because they actually taste alike.

Chapter 14 · The Pairing Logic

What every number in the result actually means

The score

Cosine similarity between the food's taste vector and this specific wine's vector. Taste proximity — not a quality rating. 62% can be a better pairing than 90% if you want contrast.

cosine(food_vec, wine_vec)
The wine

Not the same wine for every dish in the cluster. Cheesecake and pizza are both "something rich" — but their vectors point differently, so they get different wines from the same pool.

ranked per food vector, not cluster centroid
Flavor tags

The words that scored highest in both the food description and the wine review. These are the specific flavor words that brought the two together.

TF-IDF overlap · food desc ∩ wine review
Safe Bet

Highest cosine similarity to this food's exact vector inside the nearest taste cluster. The obvious match — and genuinely satisfying.

argmax cos(food, wine) in cluster₁
Hidden Gem

Top-rated wine from the second-closest cluster. Similar enough to harmonize — different enough to reveal something unexpected.

top rating in cluster₂
Bold Move

The farthest wine that still passes a rating floor. Contrast by design — every bite of food refreshes the palate.

inverse cosine sim · rating ≥ floor
The wine review

A real quote from the WineSensed dataset — 824,000 reviews. Selected as the one whose language most closely matched the flavor profile the model identified in the food.

highest TF-IDF overlap with food description
Why it works

The system's human-readable justification — the specific flavor words that appear in both the food description and the wine review, showing exactly what brought them together.

food desc keywords ∩ wine review keywords

Chapter 15 · Full Architecture

One photo. Three recommendations. Here's how.

A photo arrives

You upload any dish. The system takes it from there.

The dish is identified

ResNet-50 classifies the image across 101 food categories, fine-tuned on Food-101.

76.5% top-1 accuracy
Its taste is put into words

The dish name retrieves a one-sentence flavor profile — dominant character, a surprising note, a contrast suggestion — written in the same register as wine reviews.

101 LLM-generated descriptions
Words become 512 numbers

TasteBiLSTM reads that sentence — forwards, backwards, with attention — and compresses it into a single taste vector: the food's address in a shared food-wine space.

BiLSTM + Bahdanau attention · 512-d · L2-normalised
A flavor neighborhood is assigned

Cosine similarity to 9 pre-built taste centroids names the profile and returns a confidence score: how close was this dish to its cluster?

BisectingKMeans · K=9 · silhouette 0.476 · 824k wine vectors
Three wines are chosen — three different ways

Each strategy picks from a pool of 10 pre-vetted wines per cluster, ranked against the exact dish vector — so two dishes in the same cluster still get different bottles.

Safe Bet — nearest flavor match Hidden Gem — pairs with the surprising note Bold Move — highest contrast that still works
